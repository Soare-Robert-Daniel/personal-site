Most browser memory games are flat — grids, pads, sequences. This one adds a third axis. A transparent wireframe cube floats on screen with a handful of colored artifacts suspended inside it. You get a few seconds to memorise their arrangement, the vault spins to a random orientation, and then one of the artifacts vanishes. Your job is to click exactly where it used to be.

It turns out spatial memory in three dimensions is harder than it sounds once you can no longer use screen coordinates as a crutch.

## How to play

1. Press **Start**. Five-ish artifacts appear inside the vault. You have a few seconds to study them — drag to rotate, scroll to zoom in.
2. The vault hides the artifacts and rotates to a new orientation.
3. A prompt appears like "Find the **RED SPHERE**". That artifact is the one that stays hidden; the others come back as decoys in their rotated positions.
4. Click anywhere in the scene where you think the missing artifact was. Score depends on how close the ray from your click passes to the true 3D point: **Perfect** (< 0.35), **Good** (< 0.75), **Close** (< 1.3), or miss.

Keyboard: `Space` starts and advances rounds, `R` restarts.

## How it works

There are really only three interesting ideas behind the scene.

**Everything lives under one Group.** The vault's wireframe and all the artifacts are children of a single `THREE.Group`. When it's time to shuffle, we slerp that one group's quaternion to a random orientation — nothing else has to know. Because artifact positions are stored as *local* coordinates, we get the true world position at any time with `vaultGroup.localToWorld(local.clone())`. That's the invariant that makes the scoring trivial.

**The vault uses `EdgesGeometry`.** A normal transparent box looks like a faintly-tinted cube. Wrapping the same geometry in `LineSegments(new EdgesGeometry(box), ...)` gives you that crisp holographic wireframe for free, and you can stack a near-invisible `MeshBasicMaterial` inside for a bit of depth.

```ts
const box = new THREE.BoxGeometry(3, 3, 3);
const wire = new THREE.LineSegments(
  new THREE.EdgesGeometry(box),
  new THREE.LineBasicMaterial({ color: 0x00f0ff, transparent: true, opacity: 0.65 })
);
```

**Clicks are rays, not points.** You can't pick a 3D location from a single 2D click — a click defines an infinite ray through the scene. So we don't try to collide with an invisible hitbox; we just measure how close the ray passes to the target's world position and map that distance to a score bucket. `Raycaster` does the ray setup and `ray.distanceToPoint(worldPos)` does the rest.

```ts
raycaster.setFromCamera(ndc, camera);
const world = vaultGroup.localToWorld(target.localPos.clone());
const distance = raycaster.ray.distanceToPoint(world);
```

`OrbitControls` lets you inspect the arrangement from different angles during the memorisation phase, which is also the thing that makes this interesting: the more you rotate to study, the more you have to mentally re-rotate when the shuffle lands somewhere else. Human working memory does not love that.
