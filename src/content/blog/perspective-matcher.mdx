Anamorphic art — the kind of pavement drawing that looks like a pit in the ground from exactly one spot, and like smeared nonsense from anywhere else — has always felt like the most perfectly *spatial* puzzle. This is a tiny three.js take on the same idea. The scene shows you a letter made of floating cubes for three seconds, scatters the cubes along the viewing axis, and asks you to drag-rotate the camera back to the one angle that reconstructs the shape.

## How to play

1. Press **Start**. A letter appears as a grid of cyan cubes on a faint plane. You have three seconds to memorise *which* letter it is.
2. The plane disappears and the cubes scatter forward and backward along the viewing axis. The camera tumbles off to a random oblique angle.
3. Drag the scene around. An alignment bar at the top tells you how close the current camera angle is to the "magic" angle.
4. When the silhouette looks right, press **Lock in**. Score bands are **Perfect** (≥99.9%), **Good** (≥99.3%), **Close** (≥96.6%), else miss.

Keyboard: `Space` starts / locks in / advances rounds, `R` restarts.

## How it works

The game is built around one small observation about projection.

**The cubes' local XY never changes.** Each fragment stores two positions in the sculpture's *local* frame: `solved` (on the letter's plane, `z = 0`) and `exploded` (same XY, random Z). When the scatter animation plays, the cubes slide only along the sculpture's local Z axis. Their shadow onto the sculpture's local XY plane is always identical — it always spells the letter. The silhouette reconstructs for the player exactly when the camera is looking straight down that axis. From any other angle, parallax on the Z offsets pulls each cube sideways by a different amount, and the shape dissolves into noise.

**One group, one transform.** All fragments and a faint reference plane hang under a single `THREE.Group` called `sculpture`. Each round we give the group a random quaternion — so the "magic angle" is different every time. During reveal the camera is placed at `Q * (0, 0, +d)` so the player sees the letter from that round's correct side; during explode the camera is tumbled to a new position 65°–130° away on the viewing sphere so the player always has real work to do.

```ts
sculpture.quaternion.copy(Q);                       // random each round
camera.position.copy(new Vector3(0,0,d).applyQuaternion(Q));
```

**Alignment compares two unit vectors.** Each frame we read the camera's forward and the sculpture's local `+Z` expressed in world space, and take their dot product. Perfect alignment means camera forward points *opposite* to the sculpture's local `+Z`:

```ts
const fwd = new THREE.Vector3();
camera.getWorldDirection(fwd);                      // unit forward in world
const zWorld = new THREE.Vector3(0,0,1)
  .applyQuaternion(sculpture.quaternion);           // sculpture +Z in world
const cos = -fwd.dot(zWorld);
// cos == 1 → perfectly aligned, cos == 0 → perpendicular
```

The alignment bar shows `cos` directly as a percentage, and the score bands correspond to angular tolerances of roughly 3°, 7°, and 15°.

```ts
const sculpture = new THREE.Group();
scene.add(sculpture);

for (const cell of letterGrid) {
  const cube = new THREE.Mesh(boxGeom, mat);
  cube.position.copy(cell.solved);
  sculpture.add(cube);
}
```

The little detail that sells it is the result animation: once the player locks in, the cubes slerp back to `z = 0` and the faint reference plane fades in. If the lock-in angle was close, the letter clicks into place sharply; if it was off, you get to see exactly how wrong you were as the cubes glide back through the camera.
